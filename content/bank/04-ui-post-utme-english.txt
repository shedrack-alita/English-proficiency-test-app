@tip Modal verbs carry meaning: "should" (duty), "must" (necessity or strong deduction), "may" (permission), "might" (possibility), "need not" (no necessity). Choose by meaning, then check the form.
University students ____ behave well. | can | *should | may | must || "Should" expresses what is right or expected: students ought to behave well.
Gloria ____ have finished the project two days ago. | *must | may | can | supposed to || "Must have + past participle" expresses a confident deduction about the past.
@tip "Need" as a modal has no -s and is followed by the bare infinitive in negatives: "He need not come."
He ____ not come now. | needs | needed | *need | will need || As a modal, "need" takes no -s in the negative: "He need not come".
@tip "Be supposed to" (= be expected to) always needs "be": "is supposed to", "was supposed to".
Janet ____ to know that we are all here. | suppose | supposes | *is supposed | should suppose || "Is supposed to know" means "is expected to know".
@tip After "let it be", use the past participle: "let it be known".
Let it be ____ that there was no king in Ayegun. | know | knew | *known | knows || "Let it be known" is a passive construction with the past participle "known".
@tip Modal verbs carry meaning: "may" for permission, "might" for possibility, "should" for duty.
"You ____ now see the director," the secretary told the visitors. | can | *may | should | would || A secretary giving visitors permission politely uses "may".

@tip A dangling participle is one whose implied subject is not the subject of the main clause. The person who did the -ing action must be the subject that follows the comma.

@tip A first conditional pairs a present tense with "will"; a second conditional pairs a past tense with "would". Don't mix them.

@tip The tag repeats the auxiliary of the statement with opposite polarity: positive statement, negative tag ("You are ..., aren't you?"); negative statement, positive tag.
You are Goodluck, ____? | are you? | *aren't you? | are'nt you? | don't you? || A positive statement with "are" takes the negative tag "aren't you?" ("are'nt" is misspelt).
@tip "Shall" in the statement takes "shan't" in the tag.
We shall see you soon, ____? | shall we? | *shan't we? | shouldn't we? | should we? || The auxiliary "shall" is repeated in the negative: "shan't we?"
@tip The tag repeats the auxiliary of the statement with opposite polarity.
She isn't Gloria, ____? | *is she? | isn't she? | aren't she? | doesn't she? || The statement is negative ("isn't"), so the tag is positive: "is she?"
@tip With "have" meaning "possess", British English may use "haven't" in the tag; "don't we?" is also common.
We have a bus, ____? | didn't we? | *haven't we? | doesn't we? | hasn't we? || "We have" (present) takes the tag "haven't we?" in British usage; past or third-person forms do not fit.
@tip When the statement has no auxiliary, use do/does/did in the tag.
You missed the match, ____? | don't you? | *didn't you? | hadn't you? | doesn't you? || "Missed" is past simple without an auxiliary, so the tag uses "did": "didn't you?"
@tip Imperatives take "will you?" in the tag.
Walk the dog right now, ____? | walkn't you? | *will you? | shall you? | would you? || An imperative (command) takes the tag "will you?"
@tip After "I am", the negative tag is "aren't I?"
I am a student, ____? | I'm I? | *aren't I? | isn't I? | wouldn't I? || The standard negative tag for "I am" is "aren't I?"

@tip An unreal present condition ("If I were ...") takes "would" + base verb in the main clause.
If I were the president, I ____ ask for the money. | will | should | *would | can || "If I were" introduces an unreal (hypothetical) condition, so the main clause uses "would".

@tip In a time clause about the future ("after/when/before ..."), use the present or present perfect, not "will".
I will see you after I ____ spoken with your supervisor. | might have | *have | will have | would have || After "after" referring to the future, English uses the present perfect: "after I have spoken".

@topic Word Class
@instruction Identify the grammatical name or function of the bold expression.
@tip A clause has a finite verb; a phrase does not. Name the unit by its form (clause, phrase) and its function (adverbial of time, appositive, complement).
**As soon as Joy arrived**, she started looking for food. The bold expression is an | adverbial phrase of reason | adverbial clause of reason | adverbial phrase of time | *adverbial clause of time || It contains a finite verb ("arrived"), so it is a clause, and "as soon as" introduces time.
My friend, **a brilliant lecturer**, won many prizes. The bold expression is a(n) | adjectival clause | relative clause | *appositive phrase | subject of "won" || It renames "my friend" and has no verb, so it is an appositive phrase.
I passed very well **because** I studied hard. The bold word is a | relative clause introducer | linking verb | adverb of reason | *subordinating conjunction || "Because" introduces a dependent (subordinate) clause, so it is a subordinating conjunction.
Bello and I love **each other**. The bold expression is a | preposition | *reciprocal pronoun | noun phrase | adjectival phrase || "Each other" expresses a mutual relationship: a reciprocal pronoun.
I prefer **writing** to acting. The bold word is a(n) | verb | participle | *gerund | object || "Writing" is an -ing form used as a noun (the object of "prefer"), i.e. a gerund.
**Having finished my assignment**, I started watching television. The bold expression is a(n) | infinitive clause | gerund | *participial phrase | noun clause || It is introduced by the participle "having finished" and modifies "I": a participial phrase.
**If you leave early**, you'll meet Sola at home. The bold expression is a | nominal clause | verbal clause | *conditional clause | adjectival clause || A clause introduced by "if" that states a condition is a conditional clause.
**Wounded by a lion**, the hunter trudged home. The bold expression is a | nominal clause | verbal clause | *participial clause | adjectival clause || It begins with the past participle "wounded" and has no finite verb: a (non-finite) participial clause.
I'm proud **of you**. The bold expression functions as | prepositional complement | *adjectival complement | subject complement | verbal complement || "Of you" completes the meaning of the adjective "proud", so it is an adjectival complement.
A noun phrase lacks one of the following: | noun | *verb | adjective | adverb || A noun phrase is built around a noun and may contain adjectives and adverbs, but it has no verb.
Eve gave Adam **an apple**. "An apple" in the sentence functions as | subject of the verb "gave" | *direct object of the verb | indirect object of the verb | object complement || "An apple" is the thing given (direct object); "Adam" is the receiver (indirect object).
I wonder if you know where he lives. This sentence is a(n) | thoughtful statement | polite command | *indirect question | subtle exclamation || It reports a question inside a statement ("I wonder if ..."), which is an indirect question. (The PDF's key gives "thoughtful statement".)
An example of a gradable adjective is | each | *beautiful | dead | live || Gradable adjectives can be intensified or compared ("very beautiful", "more beautiful"); "dead" and "live" are not normally graded.
A clause differs from a phrase because a clause contains | a larger number of words | a fewer number of words | *a finite verb | a non-finite verb || A clause has a finite verb (with tense and subject); a phrase does not.
